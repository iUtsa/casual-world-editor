You can picture it.

A block on a ramp. Two planets passing too close. A circuit you sketched, a reaction you’re curious about. Ideas start as pictures in your head, and a still picture, a diagram or a formula can only show the first frame. Turning it into something you can actually test usually takes equipment, code or a lab.

Casual World Editor runs the idea for you, then lets you change it and run it again. Change the angle, swap the planet, make the table icy. You see what happens, and you see the number that proves it.

  • How long until it reaches the bottom?
  • Does a heavier block get there sooner?
  • What changes if the ramp is twice as steep?
  • Would it slide at all on the Moon?

Four ways to ask “what if?”

Scan your desk

Use the laptop webcam or your phone. Every object is lifted into 3D with size, mass, friction and what it rests on. Orbit a photo-textured digital twin, click anything to inspect it, then push it, throw at it or change gravity.

“throw a baseball at the mug”

Snap a diagram

Photograph a textbook page or a whiteboard. A local vision model reads it and shows you an editable reading card, so you can fix anything it got wrong before the simulation runs.

“what if the angle was 60°?”

Ask the Studio

Type an experiment in plain English. Collide whales, swap Jupiter for the Moon, wire an RC circuit, burn methane or watch GPT-2 think. Each lab uses a real solver, not an animation.

“what if there was no Sun?”

Use your hand

Hold your hand up to the camera and push the digital twin with your real fingers. 21 tracked bones become physics, so you can push, tip and flick things live, then replay it exactly.

    Your hand is the physics.

    Walk-around 3D scan

    Circle the desk once. Get real shapes back.

    Record a short video walking around the table. The app solves where the camera was in every frame from the video alone (our own structure-from-motion with bundle adjustment), finds what rises above the table by parallax, then carves each object into a textured mesh with its own mass.

    1. Track 56 keyframes, camera positions within ~1 cm
    2. Find objects by plane + parallax, outlined by SAM 2.1
    3. Carve label-consistent voxels into meshes and convex hulls

    On our test desk: 5 of 6 objects found; surface error 7–11 mm; about 5 minutes on an 8 GB M1.

    Studio: every lab in one place

    Not just desks. Each lab is backed by the solver a scientist would reach for, and every result can be followed up with another “what if”.

    Rigid bodies at real-world scale

    • “two blue whales collide at 10 m/s”
    • “a toy car pulls a truck”
    • “line up 10 dominoes and knock them over”

    Under the hood MuJoCo with real sizes and masses. Toys scale properly: mass ∝ k³, speed ∝ √k. Measurements come straight from simulator state at 60 Hz.

    Orbits with real ephemerides

    • “what if Jupiter orbited Earth?”
    • “the Sun falls into a black hole”
    • “Milky Way and Andromeda collide”

    Under the hood REBOUND IAS15, conserving energy to about 1e-15, started from NASA JPL Horizons positions cached for offline use. Tidal disruption, gravitational-wave inspiral and merger physics included.

    Circuits solved like SPICE does

    • “9 V battery with two bulbs in parallel”
    • “RC circuit with 100 µF and 1 kΩ”

    Under the hood Modified nodal analysis, the method SPICE uses, with transients. Kirchhoff's laws are checked on every result.

    Reactions with measured energies, quantum chemistry when there are none

    • “burn methane”
    • “hydrogen and oxygen make water”

    Under the hood Exact balancing, measured NIST/CRC enthalpies, GFN2-xTB molecular dynamics, and DFT (M06-2X/def2-TZVP in PySCF) for molecules with no measured data, reported with an honest error bar.

    Hand control, without the mess

    MediaPipe tracks your hand in the browser at about 13 ms per frame. Pinch to grab and throw, swipe to push, or switch on 🖐 Touch and push with your fingers directly. Your hand moves the object's digital twin, not the real one, so you can knock the mug over without spilling your coffee.

    Each action picks up where the last one settled

    The scene is checkpointed after every action, so “now drop a ball on it” continues from the settled result instead of replaying the whole history. That takes 0.4 to 1.3 s per action.

    1. scan
    2. knock over the bottle
    3. moon gravity

    Every answer is checked

    Results come with the measured value next to the textbook prediction and the agreement between them. The tests check physics, not just code paths: a resting contact force must equal the object's weight, and energy given to a passive object must equal the energy the other one gave up.

    Your desk stays on your desk

    Everything runs on your laptop. Camera frames, voice and diagrams never leave the machine. The phone camera talks to the laptop over your local network, on HTTPS with a certificate generated on first run.

    Cloud features exist but are off by default. With ANTHROPIC_API_KEY set, explanations get more fluent; without it, a deterministic writer produces the same structure offline. With FAL_KEY, objects missing from the library can be generated as 3D models, and only the text prompt is sent.

    What it can't do yet

    Better to know before the demo. Close other heavy apps first: 8 GB is tight.

    Quick scans guess the back

    A one-photo scan infers hidden sides from simple shapes. The walk-around scan sees them, but takes about 5 minutes on 8 GB.

    Scale can drift

    A single camera can misjudge size. Calibrate with one object's real height, or use RGB-D input.

    Keep the camera still

    There's no continuous tracking after a scan yet. The phone notices movement and asks for a rescan.

    Some objects slip through

    On our test desk the walk-around scan missed a small ball and carved away a thin clock. We list what it found so you can check.

    Scan numbers are from a test desk

    The walk-around accuracy above was measured on a photoreal synthetic desk with known answers. Real-phone sweeps are next.

    Touch pushes, it doesn't grip

    Fingers push, tip and flick by contact. Picking something up needs a grip assist we haven't built yet; pinch-to-grab covers it for now.

    Hand size is estimated

    🖐 Touch scales your hand from MediaPipe's model, so very large or small hands touch slightly early or late.

    Small models misread

    Messy handwriting or odd objects can trip the local vision model, which is why the reading card is editable.

    DFT runs a little low

    About 3–4% on large combustion reactions. The reported uncertainty covers it.

    Run it on your Mac

    Requires macOS on Apple Silicon, Python 3.12 and Node 18+. No keys, no account.

    Get the code
    git clone https://github.com/iUtsa/causal-world-editor.git
cd causal-world-editor
    Set up the app: Python env, ~250 MB of vision models, UI build
    ./setup.sh
.venv/bin/pip install rebound reboundx pyscf \
  rdkit tblite sympy scipy sentence-transformers \
  qrcode trimesh pillow
    Add the local vision model (Qwen2.5-VL-3B on the Apple GPU)
    python3.12 -m venv .venv-ai
.venv-ai/bin/pip install mlx-vlm
    Start it, open localhost:8000 and press Get started
    ./restart.sh

    Want the phone as a camera? Click Connect phone in the app and scan the QR code.
